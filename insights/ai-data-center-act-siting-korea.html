Capital Region or Not: How Korea's AI Data Center Act Reshapes Siting Diligence

Second in our data center series. The previous article showed a scorecard that structurally penalizes capital-region sites. Korea's new AI data center legislation goes further: for projects outside the capital region it removes the assessment altogether, compresses permitting, and opens a direct route to renewable supply. From March 2027, where a data center sits is a regulatory position — and diligence has to read it as one.

Published 19 November 2026 · Dime Works · Reading time ~7 min

Key takeaways

  • The Act on the Promotion of the AI Data Center Industry passed the National Assembly in May 2026, was promulgated in June 2026, and takes effect on 10 March 2027. Its relief is geographic by design: non-capital-region projects only.
  • Four instruments matter for a deal: exemption from the power grid impact assessment for non-capital-region AI data centers; an integrated permitting route through the science ministry with a deemed-approval clock; a direct power purchase channel from renewable generators outside the wholesale market; and designated AI data center special zones tied to distributed-energy zones.
  • Exemption from the assessment is not supply. The review disappears; the physics of the local grid segment and the utility connection agreement do not. Diligence still has to verify capacity — it just verifies it against a different document set.
  • Faster permitting means fewer independent reviews before the buyer arrives. Integrated and deemed approvals compress the windows in which environmental issues used to surface, so more of that review falls on the transaction itself.
  • Non-capital-region sites bring their own ESG scope — greenfield land, water availability, coastal cooling proposals, community acceptance. The scope moves; it does not shrink.

What the Act does, in four instruments

The legislation is a promotion statute rather than an environmental one, and its design intent is plain from its geography: it offers relief to AI data center projects outside the Seoul capital region and none to those inside it. Read for transaction purposes, four provisions carry the weight.

Why the geography is not incidental

The Act is best read alongside the capital-region numbers: 522 cumulative grid assessment filings, 10 final approvals, 1.9% of applications, as of March 2026. Rather than expand capital-region supply, the legislation redirects demand. That has an immediate consequence for a target already sited in the capital region — it inherits the bottleneck in full and receives none of the relief — and a second-order consequence for one sited outside it: the value of the location now includes a regulatory exemption whose scope, timing, and durability are themselves diligence questions.

It also interacts with the assessment's own scoring. As the previous article set out, roughly 40% of that scorecard is non-technical — regional underdevelopment, local power self-sufficiency, support-program status. A non-capital-region project that does not qualify for the Act's exemption still enters the assessment with a structural advantage on those items, and the implementing notice that took effect in November 2026 sharpened the gradient: capital-region data centers now need 75 points to pass, against 70 elsewhere. The Act and the scorecard push the same way.

Exemption is not supply

The most common error we expect from this legislation is to treat "exempt from the grid impact assessment" as "power secured." The assessment is a screening layer the government placed in front of the utility connection to manage where load lands. Removing it for a qualifying project restores the older sequence — the developer deals with the utility on capacity, connection point, and timing — but it does not manufacture headroom where the local grid segment has none. Non-capital-region substations vary widely in available capacity, and a region chosen for its exemption may or may not have the physical supply the project assumes.

For diligence, that means the power workstream on an exempt project shifts document sets rather than disappearing: from the assessment filing and its stage to the utility's connection agreement, its capacity and timing terms, and any conditions on phasing. The questions are the same ones the opening article put on the table — what capacity is confirmed, by whom, as of when — asked of a different counterparty.

Faster permitting moves review onto the buyer

Integrated permitting with a deemed-approval clock is designed to shorten the calendar, and it will. Its side effect is that the individual permits which used to be reviewed one at a time — each with its own authority, its own submission, and its own opportunity to surface an environmental issue — are processed together, and can be deemed approved by the passage of time. A permit obtained that way is valid, but it is thinner as evidence: less has been looked at by someone other than the applicant.

The practical consequence is that a larger share of the environmental review shifts from the permitting record onto the transaction. On a consolidated approval, diligence should reconstruct which permits were absorbed, which conditions attached, and what each absorbed permit would ordinarily have required — the discipline set out in our guide to ESG due diligence on unbuilt assets, where the permit dossier is the evidence base because the building is not there yet.

The non-capital-region scope is different, not smaller

Relocating the asset class does not relocate the ESG review to easier ground. It changes the ground. Sites outside the capital region are more often greenfield or converted agricultural and industrial land, which raises the land-history and soil questions that a repurposed capital-region building rarely did. Water availability moves to the front of the file where cooling design depends on it, a subject we take up later in this series. Coastal siting — increasingly proposed on the strength of seawater cooling and the exemption alike — brings intake, discharge, and thermal questions of its own. And community acceptance, a scored item in the grid assessment and a live one in any special-zone designation, is part of the social review under an Equator Principles or IFC-based scope rather than a public-relations matter beside it.

A capital-region facility with a confirmed grid connection and a non-capital-region project with an exemption and a special-zone designation are both financeable. They are not the same diligence.

What to verify, and when

  1. Qualification. Does the project fall within the Act's definition of an AI data center, and within the exemption's scope once subordinate rules issue? A general-purpose data center may not.
  2. Timing. Where does the deal calendar sit relative to 10 March 2027? A project that files under the current regime and one that waits for the Act are on different tracks.
  3. Supply. Independent of any exemption: what has the utility confirmed, in what document, with what capacity and timing?
  4. Permits. On an integrated approval, which permits were absorbed and which conditions attach; on a deemed approval, what was never reviewed.
  5. Zone status. Designated, candidate, or aspirational; support committed versus support enabled.
  6. The relocated scope. Land history, water, coastal interface, and community acceptance — scoped for the site actually proposed.

Scope limitations and uncertainty
