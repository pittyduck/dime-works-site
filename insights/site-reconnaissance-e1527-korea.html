Site Reconnaissance Under ASTM E1527-21: How to Walk a Korean Site

Third in the methodology series. Records can be thin and databases can be silent — the site itself cannot hide as easily. The reconnaissance is where a Phase I earns its fee, and E1527-21 treats it as a structured search, not a guided tour. Here is the discipline, its Korean particulars, and the walkthrough failures we keep seeing in other people's reports.

Published 31 August 2026 · Dime Works · Reading time ~6 min

Key takeaways

  • The reconnaissance exists to find evidence of releases and likely releases — the raw material of the REC judgment — through systematic coverage of the site, its interiors, and its adjoining context.
  • Coverage is the method: every accessible area walked, limitations documented. What could not be seen is a required finding, not an awkward footnote.
  • On Korean sites the highest-yield territory is specific: floor trenches and sumps, the backs of buildings where drums accumulate, roof and mezzanine tanks, and the seam where the site meets its dense neighbors.
  • Interviews carry more weight in Korea than the standard assumes — where records are thin, the twenty-year maintenance manager is frequently the best historical database on the property. Structure the conversation accordingly.
  • The repeated failures: escorted-route-only walks, no interior access to the oldest building, verbal-only interviews, and observations recorded without location or photo discipline — unusable when the finding is challenged a year later.

What the standard actually asks for

  • A search image, not a stroll. The walk looks for defined things: staining and stressed vegetation, storage of hazardous substances and petroleum products, tanks and their vents and fill ports, drains and sumps and where they lead, transformers, odors, fill and grading anomalies, wells. The assessor arrives with the site history read and the working model in mind — the reconnaissance tests it against the physical site.
  • Interiors and periphery, not just yards. Process buildings from the inside, storage areas, and the adjoining properties as observable from the site — releases respect none of those boundaries.
  • Limitations on the record. Locked rooms, snow cover, no roof access, an escort who wouldn't unlock the old warehouse — E1527-21 requires these to be stated as limitations. The unseen area is a data gap the report must own.
  • Interviews as evidence. Current owners, operators, and occupants are interview subjects under the standard — their answers are findings, corroborated where possible, contradictions noted.
  • Contemporaneous documentation. Notes, photographs, locations — captured during the walk, not reconstructed from memory in the hotel.

Where it meets the Korean site

  • The records asymmetry raises the stakes. Because a Korean desktop non-finding is weaker evidence than its US counterpart — the gap discussed in our E1527-21 analysis — the reconnaissance carries proportionally more of the Phase I's evidentiary weight. A perfunctory walk hurts a Korean Phase I more than it would hurt a US one.
  • Know the Korean high-yield spots. Floor trenches under process lines (the plating industry's confession booth), the informal drum yard behind the oldest building, patched slab sections over former pits, mezzanine and rooftop day tanks that appear on no drawing, and the boundary seam with the immediately adjacent operation — dense siting means a neighbor's problem is often visible from your side of the wall.
  • Interview like it matters. The long-tenured maintenance manager, the retired plant engineer kept on part-time, the security guard of twenty years — on Korean family-run industrials these people hold what the file room doesn't. Interview in Korean, on the floor rather than in the conference room, with the old drawings open; note where testimony and documents disagree. "There used to be a tank behind that wall" has launched more justified Phase II escalations than any database line.
  • Watch for the regulated objects. A tank spotted on the walk is not just a REC candidate — it may be a designated facility with a statutory inspection history to request the next morning. The reconnaissance feeds the Korean document chase as much as the ASTM judgment.

The mistakes that repeat

  1. The escorted-route walk. Seeing exactly what the host planned to show. Coverage means requesting the areas not on the route — and recording it as a limitation when the answer is no.
  2. Skipping the oldest interior. The newest building is clean and open; the 1985 warehouse is locked and "just storage." The old interior is precisely where the site's history is physically stored.
  3. The unstructured interview. Twenty minutes of pleasantries with the site manager, no names, no specifics, nothing attributable. Prepared questions, identified interviewees, recorded answers — or it isn't evidence.
  4. Undisciplined documentation. Photos with no location key, notes typed up a week later. When a finding is disputed in a deal negotiation — and good findings are — the contemporaneous record is what holds.
  5. Walking without the history. Reconnaissance before the records review inverts the method: you can only recognize the anomaly if you know what the site is supposed to look like. Read first, then walk.

Scope limitations and uncertainty

This article is general information, not legal advice, and describes the reconnaissance discipline of ASTM E1527-21 at a conceptual level in our own words — it does not reproduce the standard's text, and the standard controls its own requirements. Site-specific assessments require qualified environmental professionals.
