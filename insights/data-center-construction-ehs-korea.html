Building a Data Center in Korea: The Construction-Phase EHS an Investor Inherits

Sixth in our data center series. Much of the capital now moving into Korean data centers arrives before the building does — forward purchases, development joint ventures, and pre-let structures that commit an investor to an asset that is, at signing, a site with a permit and a contractor. The environmental and safety record of the eighteen to thirty months that follow is written by that contractor and inherited by that investor. This article sets out what the record contains, and what a buyer should be reading in it before and during the build.

Published 19 November 2026 · Dime Works · Reading time ~7 min

Key takeaways

  • Korean construction carries a standing set of environmental filings that any data center build triggers: pre-notification of noise- and vibration-generating work from 1,000 square metres of building floor area, fugitive-dust registration, and construction-waste and spoil obligations. Their presence and timing are the first thing a buyer's file should show.
  • Safety planning obligations scale with height and excavation depth, and a data center — heavy structure, deep services, large mechanical and electrical installation — routinely triggers the deep-excavation and large-structure thresholds. Korea's serious-accident legislation makes the consequences personal for management.
  • The generator, fuel-storage, and cooling-plant permits described earlier in this series are obtained during construction. A build that does not sequence them correctly hands the owner a facility that is complete and not yet lawful to operate.
  • Commissioning is operation: generator load tests, cooling-plant start-up, and 24-hour work windows generate the noise, discharge, and community complaints that define the facility's neighbourhood position from day one.
  • A forward-purchase buyer should treat construction-phase oversight as a diligence function with a calendar, not a single pre-signing review — the discipline we set out for unbuilt assets generally, applied to an asset that is mostly plant.

Why the build is the file

On an operating asset, the environmental record is an accumulation the buyer reads. On a forward purchase, the record is being created during the deal, by a party the buyer did not select and cannot direct. Our guide to ESG due diligence on unbuilt assets set out the general method — the permit dossier as evidence base, conditional findings, a construction EHS review layered on top. A data center sharpens each element, because the building is a shell around plant: the environmental substance of the asset — standby generation, fuel storage, cooling, electrical — is installed, permitted, and commissioned during construction rather than inherited from a previous use.

The plant is permitted during the build

The permits that define an operating data center's environmental position — the air-emission filing for the generator bank, the specified soil-facility registration for the diesel storage, the dangerous-substance storage permit, the cooling-water and discharge arrangements — are described in our articles on backup power and water and cooling. On a new build, every one of them is obtained during construction, usually by the contractor or its subcontractors on the owner's behalf, on a schedule driven by installation rather than by the owner's operating plan.

That sequencing is where forward-purchase buyers get hurt. A fuel system installed before its storage permit, a generator bank commissioned before its emissions filing, a cooling tower started up before its discharge route was settled — each is a facility that is physically complete and not yet lawful to operate, and each is a finding the buyer inherits at completion. The construction EHS review should carry a permit calendar that tracks installation milestones against filing dates, so that the gap is visible before it closes on the owner.

Commissioning is operation

A data center is commissioned loudly. Generator load testing runs the full standby bank, often repeatedly and sometimes at night; cooling-plant start-up puts water through towers and drains for the first time; integrated systems tests run the facility as if it were live. All of this happens while the site is still, on paper, a construction site — and while the neighbourhood is forming its view of the facility. Korea's noise framework sets stricter limits at night, complaints are recorded, and a site's community-acceptance position — a scored item in the grid assessment and a substantive one under any social review — is being set by how commissioning is run. The buyer should know the commissioning plan, its hours, and its noise and discharge controls before it is executed.

Converting an existing building

Where the project converts an existing structure — a former industrial or logistics building, a pattern the AI data center legislation explicitly contemplates for non-capital-region sites — the build begins with demolition and strip-out, and the asbestos framework applies before anything else does: survey obligations from modest floor-area thresholds, licensed removal, notification to the labour authority ahead of work, and clearance measurement after it. The building's prior life also reopens the soil question that a greenfield data center rarely has to ask; our asset-type survey sets out why a logistics parcel is not as clean as it prices.

What the buyer should actually do

  1. Before signing: confirm the standing filings are in place and dated against commencement; read the safety plans and the allocation of responsibility; obtain the permit calendar for the plant and check it against the installation schedule.
  2. During the build: review at defined milestones — excavation complete, plant installed, commissioning — rather than once. Read the noise, dust, waste, and incident records as they accumulate, not as a bundle at completion.
  3. At commissioning: confirm every operating permit is in force before the equipment it governs is run; review the commissioning plan for hours and controls; capture the complaint record.
  4. At completion: reconcile the as-built plant against the permits, close every conditional finding, and convert what remains into the contract — the subject of the last article in this series.

Scope limitations and uncertainty

This article is general information, not legal advice. Construction-phase obligations are summarized at a general level: the noise and vibration pre-notification thresholds are stated as set out in the framework's implementing rules; fugitive-dust registration, construction-waste, safety-planning, and asbestos thresholds are described by the parameters that frame them rather than by figure, and the applicable values should be confirmed against current Korean-language texts for the specific project. Article numbers are deliberately not cited. The characterization of permit-sequencing risk and commissioning practice reflects our construction EHS experience and is labelled as such. Site-specific conclusions require an engagement, not an article.
