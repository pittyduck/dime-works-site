Signed or Aspirational: Verifying Renewable Supply at a Korean Data Center

Third in our data center series. Once grid access is established, the ESG question a data center's investors and tenants ask most is what the power is. In Korea that question has five possible answers, arranged along a spectrum from a surcharge on the utility bill to a physical connection to a generator — and a facility's public renewable claim tells you nothing about which one it holds. This article sets out the instruments, what each actually delivers, and how diligence separates a signed position from a stated intention.

Published 19 November 2026 · Dime Works · Reading time ~8 min

Key takeaways

  • Korea's corporate renewable framework offers five routes: the green premium surcharge, certificate purchase, third-party PPA through the utility, direct PPA with a generator, and on-site generation. They differ in cost, tenor, physical delivery, and whether they count as an emissions reduction.
  • The green premium accounts for the overwhelming majority of implementation by volume — reported at more than 99% — because it is cheap and simple. It is also the route that does not qualify as a greenhouse-gas reduction under the national emissions trading rules. A claim resting on it is a procurement fact, not a decarbonization one.
  • Direct PPAs are the instrument lenders and global tenants increasingly expect. They carry network charges and settlement costs on top of the energy price, run long, and are the hardest to originate — which is why so many data center "renewable" positions are letters of intent rather than contracts.
  • The new AI data center legislation opens a direct supply channel from renewable generators to qualifying operators outside the wholesale market — a sixth route that depends on the Act being in force and the project qualifying.
  • Diligence should establish four things for every claim: the instrument, the executed document, what it physically delivers to this site, and who holds it — landlord, tenant, or a parent whose commitment does not bind the asset.

Five instruments, not one claim

A data center operator, a hyperscale tenant, and a fund's ESG policy can each say the facility is "powered by renewables" while holding entirely different things. Korea's framework for corporate renewable use — administered as the domestic RE100 implementation scheme — recognizes five instruments. Placed side by side, their differences are the diligence agenda.

InstrumentWhat it isWhat it delivers, and what it doesn't
Green premiumA voluntary surcharge on the utility tariff — reported at roughly 10 won per kWh — in return for a renewable-use confirmation issued by the utility.Cheapest and simplest; the overwhelming majority of implementation volume. No physical link to any generator, and not recognized as a greenhouse-gas reduction under the national emissions trading scheme. A procurement statement, not a decarbonization one.
Certificate purchaseBuying renewable energy certificates from generators to cover the shortfall in renewable use.Flexible in volume and timing; recognized as a reduction. Exposed to certificate market structure and pricing, and to policy changes affecting the spot market. Still no physical delivery tie.
Third-party PPAA contract in which the utility intermediates between the generator and the corporate buyer — an arrangement that pre-dated the direct PPA legislation and remains available.Recognized as a reduction; a contract rather than a surcharge. The utility sits in the chain, with the pricing and terms that implies.
Direct PPAA long-term contract between the corporate buyer and a renewable generator, with the utility's network carrying the power.Recognized; hedges tariff-increase risk; the instrument lenders and global tenants increasingly expect. The price stacks energy, network usage charges on the PPA volume, and demand-based charges; origination and contracting are the hardest of the five.
On-site generationRenewable capacity on the facility's own roof or land.Physically delivered, no network charges, cheapest over time; capital-intensive and capacity-limited — a data center's roof does not meaningfully offset a data center's load.

Two structural points follow from the table. First, the instrument that dominates by volume is the one with the weakest decarbonization standing — so a site that reports full renewable coverage on the green premium has, in substance, paid a surcharge. Second, the instruments that satisfy a demanding lender or tenant are the ones least likely to already exist on a project under diligence, because they take longest to put in place. That gap is where "aspirational" lives.

The AI data center channel

The legislation described in the previous article adds a route specific to this asset class: renewable generators may supply qualifying AI data center operators directly, outside the wholesale electricity market. For a project that intends to rely on it, three dependencies attach that a conventional direct PPA does not carry — the Act must be in force (from 10 March 2027), the project must qualify under its definitions, and the arrangement must be structured under the special provision rather than the general framework. A renewable plan that assumes this channel before those three are settled is a plan, not a supply position.

Four things to establish for every claim

  1. The instrument. Which of the routes above — named precisely. "RE100-compliant" or "100% renewable" is not an answer; it is a prompt for one.
  2. The document. An executed contract with a counterparty, a term, a volume, and a price — as distinct from a memorandum of understanding, a term sheet, a procurement policy, or a parent-company commitment. Each of those has a place in a data room; none of them is supply.
  3. The delivery. What physically reaches this site. A green premium or certificate position delivers nothing to the facility; a PPA delivers through the network; only on-site generation delivers behind the meter. The distinction decides how the claim can be described to lenders and how it survives a change in the certificate or tariff framework.
  4. The holder. Whether the position sits with the landlord, the operator, a tenant, or a parent. A hyperscale tenant's global renewable commitment does not bind the building it leases; a landlord's PPA may not follow the asset on a sale. Who holds the contract determines whether it is an attribute of the asset or of a counterparty who may leave.

Reading the claim against the load

Data centers run flat, around the clock; most renewable generation in Korea does not. A supply position should therefore be read against the facility's load profile as well as its annual total. An arrangement that covers the site's annual consumption on paper may cover a fraction of it hour by hour, with the balance drawn from the grid at the grid's emissions intensity — a point global tenants with hourly-matching commitments now raise directly. Diligence does not need to resolve the accounting method, but it should record which method the claim uses, because a claim that is true on an annual basis and untrue on an hourly one will be described differently by different stakeholders in the same deal.

The same discipline applies to the emissions statement built on the supply position. Market-based and location-based accounting can produce different figures for the same facility; the position an investor prices should be the one that survives the stricter reading, and the file should show both. Our carbon and energy due diligence guide sets out the general method; on a data center, the supply contract is the document it is applied to.

What this changes in the report

On most assets, renewable procurement is a paragraph. On a data center it is a section with its own findings, because the answer drives both the ESG narrative and the operating cost. The report should state, for each claim: the instrument; the executed document or its absence; the physical delivery; the holder; the tenor and volume against the load; and the accounting basis. Where the answer is "letter of intent" or "parent commitment," that is a finding, not a footnote — and one that the contract stage of the transaction, which we take up at the end of this series, has to convert into an obligation or price.

Scope limitations and uncertainty

This article is general information, not legal, investment, or tax advice. The instruments are described at the level of the domestic corporate renewable framework as reported in Korean industry and advisory sources; the green premium rate, the reported share of implementation by volume, and the treatment of each instrument under the national emissions trading rules are attributed to those sources and are subject to periodic change. Direct PPA cost components are summarized from industry commentary on the framework's structure rather than from any specific contract. The AI data center supply channel is described from the Act as promulgated in June 2026, not yet in force and with subordinate legislation outstanding. Article numbers are deliberately not cited; Korean-language texts control. Site-specific conclusions require an engagement, not an article.
